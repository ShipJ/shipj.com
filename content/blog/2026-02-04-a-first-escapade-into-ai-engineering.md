I recently applied for a *‘data engineering lead’* role, and job description was wild. Data engineering, systems architecture, analytics engineering, data science, stakeholder management, and what sounded like some AI work all rolled into one. I hesitated when speaking with the recruiter because the scope of the role felt extremely broad. It seemed less of a defined position and more like a wishlist of everything the company might need.

I was encouraged to apply as *“the team was interested in my profile”*.

Early rounds were great and the turnaround was quick. Conversations were engaging, and the process felt well-organised - fast-paced but within a week I’d completed the first three interviews.

I was not given a take-home task. Instead, I had a 90-minute technical interview with three members of the team, including the hiring manager. I was tested on ETL design, dbt and LookML basics, data modelling, stakeholder trade-offs, and how I’d approach building scalable data systems. It was a broad technical conversation, but I felt I handled it well.

I received great feedback the next day, and at that point I felt positive. I enjoyed speaking with the team - the feedback suggested I might be capable of doing the work, and the process had moved quickly. Might this be the job offer I’d been waiting for?

Then I waited.

And waited.

And waited.

I knew there was another (final) round with the CTO, but it wasn’t possible to organise.

Three weeks later, I was told I would be having another conversation. This was already a little strange, especially given that the first three interviews had all happened within a week.

The day before the meeting, it was postponed until the following day.

And then again.

On the day of the rescheduled meeting, I prepared as best I could. Based on where I was in the process, I assumed it would be more of a leadership or final-stage conversation. I reviewed my notes, thought through examples around delivery, stakeholder management, technical decision-making, and how I would help shape a function that clearly had a broad remit.

Then the meeting time came.

No one joined.

I waited.

I was already doing something else when, 15 minutes into the meeting, he showed up. There was no apology for being late. No acknowledgement of the delay. No mention of the three weeks of silence. Just a quick explanation that he had been on an “all-hands technical call”.

He began by asking about my experience with AI.

That was when the conversation started to feel disconnected from the process I had been through so far.

The job description had not clearly positioned the role as hands-on AI engineering. The technical interview had focused heavily on data engineering, ETL, LookML, systems thinking, and analytics architecture. None of the previous conversations had suggested that deep AI, LLM, or agent-building experience was the central requirement.

The following week, I received the following feedback:

I wasn’t really sure how to take that. I obviously said something to give that impression; but of all things I would have rejected me, that probably wouldn’t be it! Most people in data, engineering, or analytics are constantly learning - it’s part of the job. But there is a difference between being unwilling to upskill and not being told that a role is looking for a specific AI skillset until the final stage of the process.

Based on the earlier feedback, it seemed clear that I was capable of doing the work they had actually assessed me on. I enjoyed speaking with the team, and I came away from the technical round feeling like the role could be a good fit.

But by the end of the process, the signal had changed.

Advice to companies hiring for AI engineers: if you are looking for someone who is experienced in AI, LLMs, agents, or applied machine learning systems, make that clear in the job description.

If you are looking for someone *“willing to upskill in AI”,* then at least include the words AI, LLM, or agents somewhere in the role requirements.

Candidates can only prepare for the role you describe.

There were plenty of <u>red flags</u> in hindsight: the broad job description, the unexplained delay, the last-minute postponements, the late arrival, and the mismatch between the interview process and the feedback.

Ultimately, I think I dodged a bullet.

The job hunt continues.
