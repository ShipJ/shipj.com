### The goal

I set myself an alarmingly simple goal: 

> Improve my 5K time from **30→20 minutes** in 6 months

This is the kind of goal that begins with self-improvement and ends with me googling “*should my calves make those noises*?”. On paper, no problem - ten minutes faster. Six months. One increasingly worn out pair of running shoes.

In reality, it means going from roughly **6 minutes per kilometre** to **4 minutes per kilometre**. That is a major shift. A 30-minute 5K is controlled, conversational, and compatible with people-watching. A 20-minute 5K is more like being chased by a deadline with lungs.

### Why 20 minutes?

The 20-minute 5K has a psychological weight. It is not elite but it is undeniably fast for a recreational runner. It’s in the awkward zone where you can no longer pretend you are “just jogging,” but you are also not yet the sort of person who owns multiple pairs of carbon-plated shoes and says things like “threshold session” at dinner.

It is a clean benchmark. Four-minute kilometres. Twelve kilometres per hour. Fast enough to feel serious. Slow enough that I can still tell myself it is possible.

### Plan A: run faster

The obvious way to improve a 5K time is to go outside and run 5K as hard as possible, repeatedly, until either the time drops or the body files a formal complaint.

This is also the bad plan.

Running faster requires more than suffering. It needs aerobic capacity, leg strength, running economy, recovery, and some ability to distinguish productive discomfort from “you are about to spend three weeks cross-training on an exercise bike while sulking.”

So the plan has to be more structured.

### Plan B: the actual plan

Most runs should be easy. Annoyingly easy. Boringly easy. Easy enough that I wonder whether I am wasting my time, which usually means I am doing it correctly.

One or two sessions per week should be harder: intervals, tempo runs, or controlled efforts around target pace. These are the workouts that teach the body what faster running feels like, ideally without teaching it what injury feels like.

Strength training matters too. Not bodybuilding. Not turning into a squat rack influencer. Just enough work on calves, glutes, hamstrings, hips, and core to make the running less fragile.

Recovery is the least glamorous part, but probably the most important. Sleep, rest days, lighter weeks, and accepting that improvement does not happen during the workout. It happens afterwards, assuming I have not tried to “sneak in” an extra session because I watched one motivational video.

### The numbers are rude

- A 30-minute 5K is a 6:00/km pace.

- A 20-minute 5K is a 4:00/km pace.

That means every kilometre needs to be 2 minutes faster. Not just the final kilometre. Not just the one where the playlist gets good. All five of them. This is where the goal becomes less abstract. Taking ten minutes off a 5K is not polishing the edges. It is changing the system. The lungs need to handle more demand and the legs need to turn over faster. The mind needs to tolerate a higher level of discomfort.

## What Success Might Actually Look Like

The headline goal is 20 minutes, but progress probably will not be linear. Some weeks will feel easy. Some weeks will feel like I have forgotten how knees work. There may be plateaus. There may be regressions. There will almost certainly be at least one run where I blame the weather, the route, my shoes, breakfast, GPS drift, and the concept of time.

A good six-month block would still be a success if I become more consistent, more resilient, and significantly faster. The 20-minute barrier is the target, but the process is the point.

That is a very mature sentence, and I resent it.

## The Real Challenge

The hardest part will not be one heroic workout. It will be stacking ordinary weeks together.

Easy runs when they feel too easy. Hard sessions when they feel slightly too honest. Rest days when I want to prove something. Sensible pacing when my watch suggests I am briefly invincible.

The work is not complicated. It is just inconvenient.

### Reflections

Going from a 30-minute 5K to a 20-minute 5K in six months is ambitious. It will require structure, patience, and a willingness to spend a lot of time being humbled by a distance that looks harmless on paper.

Five kilometres is short enough to tempt arrogance and long enough to punish it.

So that is the project: six months, ten minutes, one increasingly personal relationship with discomfort.

And after that, obviously, the 10-minute 5K by **6 May 2027**.

Because if there is one thing runners are known for, it is making calm, evidence-based extrapolations.

### Extrapolating results

Of course, if I improve from 30 minutes to 20 minutes in six months, then by the iron laws of extremely bad statistics, I should be able to improve from 20 minutes to 10 minutes in the next six months.

That puts me on track for a **10-minute 5K by 6 May 2027**.

This seems plausible, provided several things happen:

First, physics resigns.

Second, World Athletics agrees to measure my 5K downhill, with a tailwind, on a travelator.

Third, my cardiovascular system is replaced with something from a small jet engine.

So yes, technically, the spreadsheet says 10 minutes. The body has not yet been consulted.
